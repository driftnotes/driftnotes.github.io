> "If you are lucky enough to have lived in Paris as a young man, then wherever
> you go for the rest of your life, it stays with you, for Paris is a moveable
> feast."
>
> — Ernest Hemingway

In December last year my girlfriend and I took the Eurostar from London St. Pancras International to Paris. Hemingway said that Paris stays with you, so I had to go see for myself.

Coasting through fields of dull green we arrived at Paris Gare du Nord, took the metro to République, and checked into the Paris France Hotel. Our balcony overlooked the Rue de Turbigo, flanked by rows of uniform windows, each adorned with their own mini balcony. Famished and at the recommendation of the concierge, I strolled to the neighbourhood bakery and picked up a ham and cheese baguette. I do not speak French, nor did the cashier speak English, so I settled with having it untoasted. As my teeth sunk into the stale bread, I tried to convince Sheena that it was good.

It was evening when we got off the metro at Trocadéro station. Turning the corner, I was greeted with one of the most magnificent views — the Eiffel Tower, sparkling yellow in all its glory, seemed to take on a life of its own in that clear night sky. We dined at an open-air table adjacent; baffled, I paid no mind to the box mac-and-cheese and cardboard steak — we were in Paris!

It drizzled the next day as we walked to the Seine. The streets, although laden with people, sang a muffled tune. Parisians walk slow, and as I came to realise, I did not need to brace for the occasional shove, frown, and tuts of red-faced passerbys. There seemed a simple, unbothered absence of urgency altogether. Quiet alleyways were a warm embrace, and the wet pavements a silky, smooth canvas on which dried and fallen leaves slept. In the spirit of immersion, we seemed to inherit that lack of haste by avoiding any semblance of a concrete itinerary. We dragged our feet as we walked the river, taking it all in. Paris is best enjoyed slow.

Over the next few days the sun began to peek through the clouds and its light bounced off pale stone and the street seemed to soften. It was also as if kitchens woke. Duck confit, baguettes (divine, this time), fries, crepes, croissants, burgers, cakes, and ramen — a gastronomic adventure I'll never forget. We sipped coffee in corner cafes, saw art I did not understand at the Louvre, and snapped portraits in photobooths. On our final night we dined at a nice restaurant to celebrate my turning 22. As we crossed a bridge on our way back to our hotel, with flowers in my hand, the Eiffel Tower sparkled at the hour mark — like a friend, saying goodbye, knowing we'll meet again.

It's been a while since that trip, but I still find myself yearning for its charm. The magic of the City of Love was not in a single monument or museum; its greatest trick was the permission to slow down: to linger over a meal, walk without purpose, and let an afternoon unfold. Paris is a moveable feast, best eaten slow.
