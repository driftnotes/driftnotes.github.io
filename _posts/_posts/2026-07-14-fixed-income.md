I start with Fixed Income because I believe it is the foundation of the pricing of all other assets.

A bond is a contract of which the holder is entitled to fixed-period coupon payments, expressed as a percentage of the notional value, in addition to the notional value itself at maturity, the expiry date of the bond. Cash flows refer to the payments the bond holder receives in each period, including the final coupon payment and notional value paid at maturity. The price of a bond is simply the present value of all remaining cash flows of the bond until maturity. We find the present value by discounting cash flows using a discount rate, which we can call r for now. Yield is sometimes used loosely, so I will be specific: the Yield to Maturity (YTM) of a bond is simply the flat discount rate, y, that solves the pricing formula of the bond, given its price. YTM is also referred to as the internal rate of return of the bond. You can also think of it as the rate of return that investors demand of a particular bond, given demand and supply equilibrium dynamics. The yield curve usually refers to the graph that plots YTM against maturity, and its shape is sometimes seen as a signal of the macro economy.

Given a bond's price and coupon rate, a rougher, intuitive approximation of yield is given by YTM = return to bondholder = sum of coupon and capital gains. Yet another price/yield relationship is given by P = Par + Par * (c-y) * PV01. Where does this come from? We know that for a bond priced at par (price = face value), coupon rate = yield. If c>y, the bond pays a higher coupon rate than the market demands, so the bond trades at a premium above par. If c<y, the bond pays a lower coupon rate than the market demands, so the bond trades at a discount below par. c-y is the rate difference, and (c-y)*par is the excess coupon payment per period in dollars. Multiplying by PV01 gives the present value of receiving that excess coupon every period for the life of the bond. Adding par gives the bond price.

YTM gives us a simple, comparable measure of the internal rate of return of a bond, but we turn to calculus to give us a measure of risk — how does price change in response to changes in yield. I find it easier to understand things by starting from the graph that plots price against yield for a given bond. What is the shape of this graph? We start by obtaining the first derivative of price with respect to yield, and that's negative, so the graph is downward sloping. The positive second derivative tells us the graph is convex.

Absolute changes in prices are not very comparable across bonds with different notionals and payoff structures, so we prefer to quote the price sensitivity in percentage terms; spelled out, that is: how much does a bond's price change in percentage terms given a 1% (100bp) change in yield? We call this modified duration, which is simply the first derivative of price with respect to yield, divided by the current price. By convention, we quote modified duration as positive, and since prices move inversely to yields, we include a negative sign. Now clearly, modified duration is the tangent of the graph, scaled inversely to price. Since the graph is convex, modified duration is a first-order, linear approximation of the price sensitivity to yield — useful for small changes in yields, but inaccurate for large moves. This concept is generally what is referred to as convexity — duration understates gains from falling yields and overstates losses from increasing yields. If you are not convinced, draw the graph for yourself. Sometimes traders like to quote price sensitivity in absolute terms, with respect to a 1bp (0.01%) change in yield. DV01 is simply the first derivative of price with respect to a 1bp change in yield, which you can express as - dP/dr * 0.0001 to scale; an alternative, seemingly roundabout formula is DV01 = modified duration * P * 0.0001. A useful application of DV01 is in curve trades where you take a directional view on the spread between a shorter and longer term yield, but would not like to have aggregate interest rate risk. You can take positions on the short and long end such that the DV01's cancel out.

Another very interesting, and related, concept to modified duration is the Macaulay duration. Formally, it is the average time for the bond holder to receive all cash flows, weighted by the present value of each cash flow as a share of the price. Intuitively, it is the "centre of gravity" of the cashflows, the average present-value-share-weighted time to receive them. It can also be thought of as the specific time from present where the price risk (price of bond changes due to change in yield) and the reinvestment risk (compounded returns from reinvested coupons change due to change in yield) cancel out. Mathematically, the modified duration is equal to the Macaulay duration divided by 1+y. They are very similar, but answer slightly different questions.

Another concept is that of spot rates. Given a bond's market price, the flat YTM is not the only rate that solves the pricing formula. In fact, YTM is merely the average, internal rate of return. Spot rates are the more accurate way to discount cash flows based on their specific payment periods, where spot rates vary with each period. It is straightforward to internalise why discount rates would vary with each period — consider two zero-coupon bonds, with differing maturities. Lending for a longer period of time introduces higher default risk; in addition, if he expects the inflation rate (not price levels itself) to increase, the lender would also want to be compensated with higher yield. There is also a liquidity premium. Hence, the spot rates are generally the more accurate way to discount cash flows to give you a bond's price. Spot rates are usually derived through bootstrapping. Suppose we are trying to derive annual spot rates. The 1-year, zero coupon bond's par rate is the 1-year spot rate. We then take a 2-year bond's pricing formula, discounting the first-year cash flow by the 1-year spot rate, and then backing out the 2-year spot rate. We repeat accordingly i.e. bootstrap to find later spot rates.

A forward rate is the implied interest rate for a future period. For example, f_1,2 would give you the implied rate of borrowing or lending between year 1 and 2. It is derived from spot rates under the no arbitrage condition. Again, the derivation is simple. We have two ways to invest money for 2 years. First, I can invest at the 2-year spot rate for 2 years, returning (1+s2)^2 at year 2. Second, I can invest for 1 year at the 1-year spot rate s1, and reinvest at the 1-year forward rate f_1,2 for the second year, returning (1+s1)(1+f_1,2) at year 2. No arbitrage locks down the forward rate. The forward rate can be locked in today through agreement, but it should be noted that it is merely an implied future rate under no-arbitrage, and is not strictly a prediction of future rates.
